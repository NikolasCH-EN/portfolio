Projects / Natural-hazard screening

Natural-Hazard Screening for U.S. Counties

A FEMA National Risk Index add-on for COLE, a team-built cost-of-living explorer, so households can screen out counties with high natural-hazard exposure.

The problem

COLE lets a household compare U.S. counties by take-home pay against household costs and climate. It had no way to screen out counties with high natural-hazard exposure.

The obvious measure, FEMA's headline Risk rating, is built from dollar losses, so it follows population. Its rank correlation with county population is 0.86, and all 17 Very High counties are among the 10% most populous. A household filtering on it would mostly be told to avoid large cities, not dangerous places.

My role

COLE is a team project. I designed and built the natural-hazard add-on: the data preparation, the ranking method, the loader checks, the interface and its tests. With no hazard limits set, COLE behaves exactly as before.

Data

  • FEMA National Risk Index (NRI) v1.20 county table, released December 2025: expected annual loss rates for each county and hazard
  • U.S. Census Bureau 2021 cartographic boundaries (1:20M) for the county map
  • In the demo, the hazard data and boundaries are real; household costs are synthetic placeholders, and a banner on every screen says so

Method

Nine hazards can be filtered: all hazards combined, hurricane, wildfire, inland flooding, coastal flooding, tornado, earthquake, winter weather and heat wave. For each one the add-on uses two NRI loss rates, under plain names:

MeasureNRI fieldMeaningRole
Building damageALR_VALB, <HAZ>_ALRBShare of building value lost per yearDrives every limit except heat wave
Risk to peopleALR_VALP, <HAZ>_ALRPExpected deaths per resident per year, each injury counted as a tenth of a deathDrives the heat-wave limit; flagged under 5,000 residents
  • Heat wave uses risk to people, because buildings are a median 0.01% of a county's expected heat loss.
  • Ranking. Each rate is ranked among COLE's own counties where that hazard causes some loss, from 0 to 100 with ties sharing the midrank, using exact arithmetic. Classes break at 20, 40, 60 and 80 (Very Low to Very High). Hurricane and coastal flooding rank only the counties they reach.
  • Deliberately not used: FEMA's headline Risk rating, for its population bias; FEMA's published loss-rate percentiles, which an audit reproduced only by weighting deaths by count instead of dollar value, so St. Louis city scores 4.7 for heat wave on them despite one of the country's highest heat-death rates; and agricultural loss, which matters to farms rather than a household's home.
  • Safeguards. Every row must match COLE on state, county name and county type, which separates pairs such as Baltimore County and Baltimore city. Missing counties stop data preparation unless they are documented gaps, and downloads are https-only with a pinned fingerprint.

What I found

Over COLE's 3,135 matched counties, for all hazards combined:

MeasureRank correlation with populationVery High in most populous 10%Very High in least populous 10%
Building damage−0.1850 of 62980 of 629
Risk to people−0.438 of 629150 of 629

Chance would put about 63 counties in each tenth. Building damage is close to independent of population, which is why it drives most limits. Risk to people leans toward small counties, where a fraction of a death per year makes a large rate, so it carries a caution under 5,000 residents.

Coverage and checks:

  • 3,135 of COLE's 3,143 counties have hazard data. The other 8 are Connecticut's legacy counties, which NRI v1.20 replaces with planning regions, so they are marked unavailable rather than approximated.
  • 131 automated tests pass, including a full load through COLE's own repository and the interface in headless Chrome.
  • An independent AI-assisted audit recomputed all 131,670 cells of the hazard table from the raw NRI file with 0 mismatches and ran 443 live queries with 0 mismatches. After the audit, an automated check confirms that all 48,812 plain-language sentences are strictly true.

What I would do differently

Next time I would bring in the National Risk Index at the Census-tract level. A county rate hides how exposure varies inside large counties such as Maricopa, and tract data would also let me estimate loss rates for the eight Connecticut legacy counties instead of marking them unavailable.

I would also run the add-on against COLE's real household data earlier. The percentiles are relative to the county list, so they shift slightly when the list changes, and I would rather see that shift during development than at merge time.
